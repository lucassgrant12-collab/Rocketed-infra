# Atlus - Business Overview

Atlus is a non custodial app that lets people spend the crypto they already hold at everyday retailers, without going through the usual process of cashing out to a bank account first. The idea came from a simple observation. Most people who hold crypto treat it purely as something to invest in or trade, not something they actually use to buy things, even though in theory it is money. The gap between owning crypto and spending crypto is what Atlus is trying to close.

The way it works is straightforward from the user's side. Someone opens the Atlus app, picks a retailer from a list of stores it already supports, such as Starbucks, Target or Uber, and pays directly from their own crypto wallet. Behind the scenes, Atlus takes that payment and buys a gift card for that exact retailer, then fills the code into the checkout automatically. The user never hands their crypto over to Atlus itself and never loses control of their funds until the moment they choose to pay, which is what makes it non custodial.

## Who the customers are

The main customer is someone who already holds crypto, has probably held it for a while, and is comfortable using a wallet, but has never really spent it on anything. This person usually only interacts with crypto through an exchange, buying and selling, and has likely never used it to actually pay for something in real life. They are not beginners who need to be taught what crypto is. They are existing holders who need a reason and a simple way to use what they already have.

A secondary customer is someone who wants to avoid the hassle and delay of manually converting crypto into cash every time they want to spend some of it, either because of the time it takes, the fees involved, or because they do not want to think about the tax implications of cashing out through an exchange every single time.

## Social impact

The main social impact Atlus could have is changing how people relate to the crypto they own. A lot of people currently see their crypto balance as just a number that goes up and down, disconnected from anything real. If people are able to spend it easily on things they actually need or want, it starts to feel more like real money and less like a number on a screen. That shift matters because it changes behaviour, not just perception.

There is a less positive side worth being honest about too. Making crypto easier to spend removes some of the natural friction that currently exists between owning it and using it. That friction is annoying, but it can also act as a kind of brake on impulsive spending. Removing that brake could make it easier for people to spend more than they intended, especially with an asset that can be volatile. This is a genuine tradeoff, not something to pretend does not exist.

## Ethics

The biggest ethical decision behind Atlus is that it never takes custody of anyone's funds. A lot of harm in the crypto industry has come from platforms holding user funds and either losing them, mismanaging them, or restricting access to them. By never holding user money, even briefly, Atlus avoids being able to cause that kind of harm in the first place. This was a deliberate design choice, not an accident.

Atlus is also upfront that it is an early, unaudited product. It does not pretend to be more secure or more finished than it actually is, and it makes clear to users that transactions cannot be reversed once sent. Being honest about limitations rather than overselling the product is treated as a basic requirement, not an afterthought.

There is still an ethical question worth sitting with. Atlus depends on third party services to actually issue the gift cards used at checkout, and those third parties run their own checks and have their own policies that Atlus does not fully control. That means there is a layer of trust being asked of the user that goes beyond just trusting Atlus itself.

## Feedback

Early feedback was gathered informally from people already involved in crypto, through direct conversation and a short survey. The general response was positive. People said the idea made sense and that they would consider using something like it if it worked well and covered stores they actually shop at.

The most interesting piece of feedback was around how people think about their crypto balance. Several people said that having a real, easy way to spend their crypto would change how they see it, making the money feel more real rather than just numbers in an app. A few specifically said this kind of product would increase the perceived value of crypto as an actual form of money, rather than just an investment or a speculative asset. That was one of the underlying assumptions the idea was trying to test, and getting that response back was a good sign the core thinking behind the product is on the right track.

## Business plan

The revenue model would most likely come from a small fee on each transaction, similar to how a normal payment processor charges a percentage. This keeps the incentive aligned with the product actually being used regularly, rather than charging a subscription fee upfront, which would be a harder sell for a new, unproven product.

Costs are mostly development time in the early stages, since the product does not require holding inventory or maintaining physical infrastructure. Once running, the main ongoing cost would be whatever margin the gift card provider charges on each purchase, plus normal software hosting costs.

The initial go to market approach would be word of mouth within existing crypto communities, since the target customer is already active in that space and is more likely to trust a recommendation from someone they already follow than an advertisement. Building trust early matters more than growing fast, especially for a product that asks people to connect a wallet and move real money.

The biggest risks are trust and coverage. Trust, because the product is new and unaudited, and people are naturally cautious about connecting a wallet to anything unfamiliar. Coverage, because the product is only useful if it works at stores people actually want to shop at, and building and verifying that list of retailers takes real time and effort. Both of these are being treated as things to solve gradually and honestly, rather than something to rush or oversell.
